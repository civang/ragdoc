A Short Tour of the Solar System

The Sun. The Sun holds more than ninety-nine percent of all the mass in the solar system. Its core fuses hydrogen into helium at roughly fifteen million degrees Celsius, and the energy released takes tens of thousands of years to work its way out to the surface. The visible surface, called the photosphere, is a comparatively cool five thousand five hundred degrees.

The inner planets. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are small, dense, and rocky. Mercury has almost no atmosphere and swings between scorching days and freezing nights. Venus is wrapped in thick carbon dioxide clouds that trap heat, making it the hottest planet at around four hundred and sixty degrees Celsius despite being farther from the Sun than Mercury. Mars is roughly half the diameter of Earth and hosts Olympus Mons, the tallest volcano in the solar system at about twenty-two kilometres high.

The asteroid belt. Between Mars and Jupiter lies a broad band of rocky debris. Despite its reputation in fiction, the belt is mostly empty space, and the total mass of everything in it is less than that of Earth's Moon. Ceres, the largest object there, is classified as a dwarf planet.

The gas giants. Jupiter is the largest planet, with a mass greater than all the other planets combined. Its Great Red Spot is a storm that has been observed for over three hundred years and is wide enough to swallow Earth. Saturn is famous for its ring system, which is made almost entirely of water ice and is remarkably thin, often only tens of metres thick despite spanning hundreds of thousands of kilometres.

The ice giants. Uranus and Neptune are colder and composed largely of water, ammonia, and methane ices. Uranus is tilted on its side, rotating at an axial tilt of about ninety-eight degrees, which gives it extreme seasons lasting decades. Neptune has the fastest winds recorded anywhere in the solar system, reaching over two thousand kilometres per hour.

Moons. There are hundreds of known moons. Jupiter's Europa is covered in ice with a liquid water ocean beneath, making it a leading candidate in the search for life. Saturn's Titan is the only moon with a substantial atmosphere and has lakes of liquid methane on its surface. Earth's own Moon is slowly drifting away from us at about three point eight centimetres per year.
