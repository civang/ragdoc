A Practical Guide to Sourdough

The starter. A sourdough starter is a live culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria kept alive by regular feeding. To begin one from scratch, mix equal weights of wholemeal flour and water and leave it at room temperature. Discard half and feed it fresh flour and water once a day. A healthy starter typically becomes reliably active after seven to ten days, doubling in volume within four to six hours of a feed.

Mixing the dough. A standard loaf uses a hydration of about seventy-five percent, meaning seven hundred and fifty grams of water for every kilogram of flour. Combine the flour and water first and let the mixture rest for an hour before adding starter and salt. This rest period, called the autolyse, allows the flour to hydrate fully and gluten to begin developing without any kneading.

Bulk fermentation. After mixing, the dough ferments as a single mass for roughly four to five hours at twenty-four degrees Celsius. Rather than kneading, perform a set of stretch and folds every thirty minutes for the first two hours. Bulk fermentation is finished when the dough has risen by about fifty percent and feels airy and domed rather than slack.

Shaping and proofing. Turn the dough out, pre-shape it into a loose round, and let it rest uncovered for twenty minutes so the surface dries slightly. Shape it tightly, place it seam side up in a floured banneton, and refrigerate overnight. This cold proof, lasting twelve to sixteen hours, develops flavour and makes the dough far easier to score.

Baking. Preheat a Dutch oven to two hundred and fifty degrees Celsius for a full hour. Score the loaf with a single decisive slash at a shallow angle, bake covered for twenty minutes to trap steam, then uncover and bake a further twenty-five minutes at two hundred and thirty degrees until the crust is deeply browned. The internal temperature should reach about ninety-six degrees.

Cooling and troubleshooting. Let the loaf cool on a wire rack for at least two hours before slicing; cutting too early leaves the crumb gummy because the starch has not finished setting. A dense loaf usually means the bulk ferment was cut short. Large irregular holes near the crust often indicate the dough was shaped too loosely and trapped a pocket of gas.
